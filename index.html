1. Neuron structures: who does what?

Behavioral neuroscientists study how biological structures and functions relate to behavior. Neurons are specialized cells that receive and transmit information.

Structure Main role Memory cue
Dendrites Typically receive incoming messages from other neurons. Receiving branches
Cell body / soma Contains the nucleus, maintains the cell, and helps integrate input. Cell's maintenance center
Axon Carries action potentials toward its terminals. Axon carries away
Terminal buttons Axon endings that release neurotransmitters at chemical synapses. Sending endings
Myelin sheath Fatty insulation around many axons that speeds signal conduction. Insulation and speed
Glial cells Support, nourish, protect, and help maintain neurons; some make myelin. Support team
Typical information flow: dendrites → cell body → axon → terminal buttons.

Hand-and-arm analogy

Fingers resemble branching dendrites; the palm resembles the cell body; the forearm resembles the axon.

Part-to-whole questions

From general to specific: neuron → axon → terminal button. A terminal button is part of an axon, and an axon is part of a neuron.

Check yourself: Which structure receives, carries, and releases?

Dendrites receive; the axon carries the action potential; terminal buttons release neurotransmitters.

2. Neural signals and synapses

Resting potential

At rest, the inside of a typical neuron is electrically negative relative to the outside, often described as approximately −70 millivolts. Resting does not mean the cell is inactive or has no electrical charge.

Action potential

An action potential is a brief electrical signal traveling along the axon. During its rising phase, the membrane depolarizes; the inside can become positive relative to the outside. It then returns toward its resting condition.

All-or-none: once threshold is reached, an action potential occurs. A stronger stimulus does not make a single action potential larger; intensity can be represented through firing rate and the activity of more neurons.

After firing, the membrane enters a refractory period. It cannot immediately produce another ordinary action potential during the absolute refractory period.

Electrical within; chemical across

In the typical chemical-synapse example used in this chapter, the action potential travels electrically along the axon, then neurotransmitters carry the signal chemically across the synaptic cleft.

  1. An action potential reaches the terminal buttons.
  2. Neurotransmitters are released.
  3. They cross the synaptic cleft.
  4. They bind to receptors on the receiving cell.
  5. The receiving neuron's probability of firing changes.

The synapse is the communication junction. The synaptic cleft is the tiny gap within it. Introductory questions often use “synapse” as shorthand for the gap.

ProcessEffect
Excitatory messageIncreases the likelihood that the receiving neuron will fire.
Inhibitory messageDecreases the likelihood that the receiving neuron will fire.
ReuptakeTransporters take neurotransmitter back into the releasing neuron.

A neuron can receive excitatory and inhibitory input at the same time. Its response depends on their combined effects.

Mirror neurons

Mirror neurons respond during a particular action and during observation of a similar action performed by another. Remember doing and observing.

The bank connects mirror neurons to empathy and language-related processes. These broader functions are research questions, not proof that mirror neurons alone explain empathy or language.

3. Neurotransmitters: high-priority associations

Learn the main association, but remember that neurotransmitters participate in multiple pathways and functions.

Neurotransmitter Chapter association Memory cue
Dopamine Movement, attention, learning, and reward-related processes. Movement and motivated learning
Acetylcholine Muscle activation and functions involving learning and memory. Action and remembering
Serotonin Mood, sleep, appetite, and other regulatory functions. Sleep, satiety, and mood
GABA A major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the adult brain. Brake
Glutamate A major excitatory neurotransmitter; important in learning and memory. Accelerator
Endorphins Endogenous opioid peptides associated with pain modulation. Internal pain relief

Disorder associations in the bank

  • Parkinson's disease → dopamine: loss of dopamine-producing neurons in a movement-related pathway.
  • Alzheimer's disease → acetylcholine: the bank emphasizes reduced cholinergic functioning.
  • Sleep, appetite, and mood → serotonin: a common chapter association.
Avoid the “one chemical = one disorder” mistake. These are learning associations, not complete explanations or ways to diagnose neurotransmitter levels from someone's symptoms. In neural communication, serotonin is a neurotransmitter; the bank's wording that calls it a hormone is imprecise.

4. Nervous system organization

Division Contains or controls
Central nervous system: CNSBrain and spinal cord.
Peripheral nervous system: PNSNerves connecting the CNS with the rest of the body.
Somatic division of the PNSSensory communication and skeletal-muscle movement.
Autonomic division of the PNSRegulation of internal organs, glands, smooth muscle, and cardiac muscle.
Sympathetic divisionAutonomic mobilization for action.
Parasympathetic divisionAutonomic support for restoration and routine maintenance.
Nervous system: central + peripheral.
Peripheral: somatic + autonomic.
Autonomic: sympathetic + parasympathetic.

Three neuron roles

  • Sensory / afferent: carry information toward the CNS.
  • Motor / efferent: carry commands away from the CNS to effectors.
  • Interneurons: connect and process information within neural circuits.

Memory cue: Afferent arrives; efferent exits.

Spinal cord and reflexes

The spinal cord is a major communication pathway between brain and body. It can also coordinate some simple reflexes: automatic responses to a stimulus.

Pulling your hand away from a hot surface can begin through a spinal circuit before conscious processing of the pain is complete. Information still travels to the brain.

Do not equate “automatic” with “autonomic.” A withdrawal reflex moves skeletal muscles through somatic pathways. A pounding heart involves autonomic regulation.

The chapter describes nervous system control as hierarchical. Remember that actual communication also involves feedback and interactions across levels.

5. Sympathetic vs. parasympathetic

Division Typical role Scenario clue
Sympathetic Mobilizes resources during challenge or threat. Pounding heart, dilated pupils, readiness for action.
Parasympathetic Supports recovery, digestion, and energy conservation. Settling down after discovering there is no danger.
Sympathetic = mobilize.
Parasympathetic = restore.

If someone sees a threatening animal, increased cardiovascular arousal points toward sympathetic activity. After the threat ends, return toward baseline points toward parasympathetic activity.

Separate the parts of an emergency response

  • Jumping out of the way: skeletal-muscle movement, involving somatic motor pathways.
  • Heart pounding: autonomic arousal, commonly sympathetic.
  • Calming afterward: parasympathetic contribution.

The bank's historical “voodoo death” item refers to extreme sympathetic activation. Treat that phrase as a historical textbook example rather than a complete account of a medical event.

6. Genetics and the endocrine system

Behavioral genetics vs. evolutionary psychology

FieldMain question
Behavioral geneticsHow do genetic and environmental differences contribute to behavioral differences?
Evolutionary psychologyHow might inherited tendencies reflect ancestral selection and adaptation?

Comparing identical and fraternal twins raised together or apart is a classic behavioral genetics approach. Genetic influence does not mean behavior is fixed or unaffected by experience.

Hormones vs. neurotransmitters

The endocrine system releases hormones into the bloodstream. Hormones influence target tissues involved in growth, metabolism, reproduction, and other functions.

In the chapter's basic contrast, neurotransmitters act across synapses; hormones travel through blood. Some chemical messengers can serve different roles in different contexts.

Gland or hormone Key association
Hypothalamus Links neural and endocrine regulation; regulates the pituitary.
Pituitary gland Located near the hypothalamus; regulates several other endocrine glands and releases growth hormone.
Growth hormone Supports growth; excess during development can contribute to unusual height.
Testosterone Contributes to sexual development and muscle-related effects; present in people of all sexes.
Oxytocin Associated with childbirth and milk ejection; produced in the hypothalamus and released through the posterior pituitary.
Melatonin Associated with daily timing and sleep-related rhythms.
Aldosterone Helps regulate sodium and potassium balance.
Erythropoietin Stimulates red blood cell production.
Anabolic-androgenic steroids Can increase muscle mass; misuse carries health and behavioral risks.
Test connections: hypothalamus → pituitary regulation; pituitary → growth hormone; bloodstream → hormones; nursing-related milk ejection → oxytocin.

The pituitary's “master gland” label is a simplification: it is regulated by the hypothalamus, and not all endocrine activity is directly controlled by the pituitary. Effects and risks of artificial hormones depend on the substance and use.

7. Brain research tools

Focus on what the tool measures or changes. Not every technique is an imaging method.

Tool What it does Recognition clue
EEG: electroencephalography Records electrical activity using scalp electrodes. Electrodes and brain-wave patterns.
MRI Produces detailed images of brain structure. Anatomy and tissue structure.
fMRI: functional MRI Uses blood-oxygen-related changes as an indirect index of activity. Functional mapping during tasks.
PET: positron emission tomography Uses a radioactive tracer to examine metabolic or other molecular processes. Tracer and biochemical activity.
TMS: transcranial magnetic stimulation Uses magnetic pulses to influence neural activity. Stimulation or temporary disruption, not a brain photograph.
Optogenetics Uses genetically introduced light-sensitive proteins to control selected cells. Genetic engineering + light.
Neuropixels probes Implanted recording probes that monitor many neurons. High-density electrical recordings.

Common scenario matches

  • Scalp electrodes while reading: EEG.
  • Seizure-related electrical activity: EEG.
  • Mapping activity near a planned surgical site: fMRI can contribute.
  • Radioactive tracer: PET.
  • Brief magnetic pulses: TMS.
  • Genetically targeted light sensitivity: optogenetics.
  • Implanted probe recording hundreds of neurons: Neuropixels.
TMS does not physically cut the brain. A temporary disruption can help test a region's contribution to a task. Repeated TMS also has clinical applications. EEG records electrical activity; it does not produce the same type of anatomical image as MRI.

8. Brainstem and cerebellum

Structure High-priority function Scenario clue
Medulla Vital autonomic functions, including breathing and cardiovascular regulation. Heartbeat and breathing.
Pons Communication among brain regions; contributes to movement and sleep-related processes. “Bridge”; the bank emphasizes coordinating movement.
Cerebellum Coordination, balance, timing, and motor learning. Stumbling, poor coordination, difficulty maintaining balance.
Reticular formation Networks involved in arousal, alertness, sleep–wake regulation, and processing incoming information. Wakefulness and background-input filtering.
Medulla = vital functions.
Pons = bridge.
Cerebellum = coordination.
Reticular formation = arousal.

The medulla, pons, and cerebellum are associated with the hindbrain. The reticular formation extends through multiple brainstem regions.

If a question describes trouble walking in a straight line, maintaining balance, or coordinating movements, cerebellum is the strongest chapter-level match.

9. Inner brain structures

Structure Main association Memory cue
Thalamus Major relay for sensory and other information traveling to cortical regions. Relay station
Hypothalamus Homeostasis, hunger, thirst, temperature regulation, endocrine control, and reproductive behavior. Internal balance
Hippocampus Formation of new declarative memories, including memories of experiences. New memory formation
Amygdala Emotional significance and emotional learning, especially threat-related learning. Emotional significance
Limbic system A traditional grouping of interconnected structures associated with emotion, motivation, and memory. Emotion, motivation, memory

Thalamus vs. hypothalamus

Routing incoming sensory information suggests the thalamus. Regulating hunger, thirst, temperature, and internal balance suggests the hypothalamus.

Homeostasis means maintaining a relatively stable internal physiological condition.

Hippocampus vs. amygdala

Difficulty forming new memories of experiences suggests the hippocampus. Emotional significance and threat-related learning suggest the amygdala.

A person who remembers many older events but cannot form new memories after surgery illustrates the chapter's hippocampal-memory distinction. The hippocampus is not a permanent storage location for every memory.

10. Cerebral cortex, lobes, and language

The cerebral cortex is the outer layer of the cerebrum. It supports sophisticated sensory processing, voluntary action, language, reasoning, and other functions through interacting networks.

Lobe Location Key chapter functions
Frontal Front Voluntary movement, planning, goal setting, inhibition, and aspects of speech production.
Parietal Upper region behind the frontal lobe Body sensation and spatial processing.
Temporal Sides near the temples Auditory processing and aspects of language and memory.
Occipital Back Visual processing.

The bank's simplified front-to-back order is frontal → parietal/temporal → occipital. The parietal and temporal lobes occupy different upper and side regions, rather than forming one straight line.

Functional areas

  • Primary motor cortex: frontal lobe; voluntary movement.
  • Primary somatosensory cortex: parietal lobe; body sensations.
  • Auditory cortex: temporal lobe; sound processing.
  • Visual cortex: occipital lobe; visual processing.
  • Association areas: integrate information and support complex cognition.

The amount of cortical representation does not simply reflect body-part size. Fine motor control and sensory sensitivity influence how much tissue is devoted to different body regions.

Executive functions and Phineas Gage

Executive functions include planning, setting goals, monitoring behavior, and inhibition. The case of Phineas Gage is associated with frontal-region injury and changes in behavior, highlighting the importance of frontal association networks.

Broca's vs. Wernicke's aphasia

Pattern Main chapter clue
Broca's aphasia Speech is effortful and nonfluent; comprehension is often relatively better preserved.
Wernicke's aphasia Language comprehension is impaired; speech can remain fluent but lack clear meaning.
Broca's: getting words out is difficult.
Wernicke's: understanding language is difficult.

These are simplified patterns. Actual language abilities depend on distributed networks and the location and extent of injury.

11. Neuroplasticity and neurogenesis

TermMeaningRecognition clue
Neuroplasticity The nervous system's capacity to change its connections, functioning, or organization. Learning-related change or reorganization after injury.
Neurogenesis Formation of new neurons. The birth of neurons, not simply new connections.
Plasticity = change.
Genesis = creation.

Strengthening or reorganizing existing neural connections is neuroplasticity even when no new neurons are created. Neuroplasticity does not guarantee complete recovery from every injury.

The bank associates adult neurogenesis with learning and memory. The extent and measurement of adult human neurogenesis remain active research questions. Avoid memorizing a universal daily neuron-production number as established fact.

12. Hemispheres, split-brain research, and biofeedback

Hemispheres and opposite-side control

The cerebrum has two hemispheres. Many motor and sensory pathways are predominantly contralateral: each hemisphere is associated with the opposite side of the body.

Lateralization

Lateralization means some functions are more strongly represented in one hemisphere than the other.

  • Left hemisphere: language is left-dominant in many people.
  • Right hemisphere: often emphasized in spatial processing and aspects of emotional tone and face processing.
These are tendencies, not exclusive assignments. Both hemispheres cooperate in normal functioning. People are not simply “left-brained” or “right-brained.”

Corpus callosum and split-brain

The corpus callosum is a large bundle of fibers connecting the hemispheres. Severing it can reduce the transfer of certain information between them. People who have undergone this procedure are often called split-brain patients.

Split-brain effects depend on the task and how information is presented; they do not mean that all ordinary daily activities necessarily become impossible.

Biofeedback

Biofeedback provides information about physiological activity so a person can learn to influence it. For example, a display of muscle tension can help someone practice relaxation.

The defining clue is feedback about bodily activity, not a magnetic pulse, brain scan, or surgical procedure.

Edition-sensitive test-bank claims to verify
  • The numerical neuron and connection items appear to target “1 trillion” and “10 quadrillion,” respectively. The bank has no marked key, and these should not be treated as universal modern estimates. Confirm the figures expected in your assigned text.
  • Corpus callosum size and language lateralization cannot reliably be predicted for an individual from sex or gender alone. Check the instructor's intended interpretation of those items.
  • The question about jumping away from a car omits the best somatic movement option. Distinguish the skeletal movement from the accompanying autonomic arousal.
  • Symptoms alone do not establish a specific neurotransmitter deficiency.
